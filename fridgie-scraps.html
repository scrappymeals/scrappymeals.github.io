Character System

Click either card to open their recurring behaviors. Scraps creates emotional energy — Fridgie gives that energy direction. Neither character speaks a language.

Voice & language model

No character dialogue, in any language. A single off-screen narrator carries the story ("Scraps couldn't resist just one more bite…"), while Fridgie and Scraps communicate entirely through sound, gesture, and expression — closer to Wile E. Coyote and the Road Runner than to a talking-animal sitcom. This makes the show translation-proof: swapping markets means recording one new narrator track, not re-casting and re-timing two characters' dialogue.

Relationship rule

Play it like a chase-partnership, not a conversation: Scraps creates the problem through action, Fridgie complicates or redirects it through a visual gag or signal, and the narrator stitches the beats together the way a storybook narrator would over silent slapstick. Either one can solve the problem, they celebrate together, and no scene should ever depend on one character verbally explaining something to the other.

Why the Narrator Model Changes the Math

The one architectural decision that makes everything downstream cheaper.

Keep the M&E stem clean, always

Every episode ships with the narrator isolated from music and effects — a clean M&E stem. That habit is what makes Phase 05 fast: a new market drops a new narrator recording into an already-final mix, instead of a full re-record and remix.

It also means animation is never touched for a new language. There's no lip sync to match, because Scraps and Fridgie never had any to begin with.

AI Technical Pipeline

The working method for turning AI-generated keyframes and storyboards into a repeatable, cost-effective episode — plus the tool stack and budget guardrails that make it airtight.

Expert Take: The Keyframe → Video Stack

You're generating keyframes/storyboards with AI, then animating them with an image-to-video model. Here's the one constraint that dominates every tool choice below: this is a recurring series, not a one-off clip — so cross-episode character and set consistency matters more than any single model's peak quality.

If you only read one box, read this one

Keyframes: Gemini "Nano Banana Pro" or Midjourney (with character reference) — both hold a locked character design across dozens of stills far better than ChatGPT's image tool, which drifts after a few edits. Use ChatGPT only for quick exploration, not final approved keyframes.

Image-to-video: Luma Ray3 as the default — first/last-frame control and up to 16 keyframes per clip is built for exactly this "board → motion" handoff. Reach for Runway Gen-4/4.5 specifically for hero shots where a single reference image has to hold a character's look across multiple different setups. Reach for Kling 3.0 when you just need the cheapest possible photorealistic-motion pass on a simple shot.

Don't subscribe to three video tools. A model-hub (Krea, Higgsfield, or fal) gives pay-as-you-go access to Luma, Runway, Kling, and Veo under one account — cheaper than three subscriptions if you're not generating daily, and it's the more cost-effective starting point for a lean team.

Voice: ElevenLabs for narrator + Scraps/Fridgie vocalizations + SFX — unchanged from the stack below.

Why not just pick the single "best" model: the AI-video leaderboard reshuffles every few weeks. What doesn't change is your shot registry, your reference bible, and your edit timeline in Resolve — that's the IP you actually own. Treat the video model as a swappable vendor, not a foundation.

AI-Native Technical Pipeline

Use AI to create controlled shots, not complete scenes. Continuity is won before video generation: fixed character references, fixed locations, approved keyframes, and one simple action per shot.

Recommended lean stack

Keyframes & boards: Gemini "Nano Banana Pro" or Midjourney (character reference) for approved final keyframes; ChatGPT image gen is fine for rough exploration only, plus optional Photoshop for surgical cleanup.

AI video: A model-hub (Krea, Higgsfield, or fal) for pay-as-you-go access to Luma Ray3, Runway Gen-4, and Kling 3.0 under one account, or Luma Plus direct if you're generating enough to justify a dedicated subscription. Use Runway Gen-4 specifically when a shot needs the same character reference to hold across very different setups.

Voice & sound: ElevenLabs Starter for narrator scratch/final VO and sound effects; use a paid tier for commercial publishing.

Edit, conform & delivery: DaVinci Resolve Free. It is the permanent production backbone; subscriptions can change, but your edit timeline, masters, stems, captions, and archive remain under your control.

Cost rule: subscribe to video tools one production month at a time. Prepare scripts, boards, shot prompts, reference packs, and animatics before activating the paid month.

Step-by-Step Technical Breakdown

The actual working method for turning AI storyboards into a repeatable Fridgie & Scraps episode.

1. Build the continuity bible once

  • Approved front, ¾, profile, back, expression, hand/paw, and scale references for Scraps and Fridgie.
  • Lock color values, materials, eye design, proportions, costume/accessories, and “never change” traits.
  • Create reusable location plates: fridge interior, kitchen counter, floor, pantry, and hero food close-up.
  • Store transparent PNG character cutouts and clean background plates separately.

2. Convert story beats into shots

  • Target 3–6 seconds per generated shot; never ask for multiple story beats in one generation.
  • Each shot gets an ID: EP01_SC03_SH020. Never rely on filenames such as “final-final-2.”
  • Define subject, action, camera, location, duration, start frame, end frame, and continuity dependencies.
  • Plan cuts around motion so small AI defects disappear at edit points.

3. Generate storyboard/keyframes

  • Generate the first frame for every shot from the same character and environment references.
  • Create a separate end frame when a shot changes pose, position, expression, or composition.
  • Approve boards for silhouette, eye-line, screen direction, prop placement, and character scale.
  • Do not move to video until the still frames cut together as a readable animatic.

4. Lock the animatic first

  • Edit stills at final shot durations with scratch narrator VO, temporary music, and essential SFX.
  • Use 24 fps for the master timeline; deliver 23.976 only when a platform or partner requires it.
  • Lock the runtime and shot order. AI video generation begins only after this gate.
  • Export a low-resolution reference movie with burned-in timecode and shot IDs.

5. Animate with image-to-video

  • Upload the approved first frame; use first/last-frame control where available.
  • Prompt motion only: actor action, camera movement, environmental motion, and timing.
  • Generate low-cost/draft passes first. Promote only approved takes to higher resolution.
  • Cap retries: three normal attempts, then simplify the shot, split it, or use a 2.5D camera move.

6. Finish outside the generator

  • Conform selected clips in Resolve; retime, stabilize, crop, mask, or freeze short problem areas.
  • Keep narrator, character vocalizations, SFX, music, and ambience on separate buses.
  • Export picture master, textless master, clean M&E, narrator stem, captions, thumbnail, and archive.
  • Archive prompts, seeds when exposed, references, source frames, selected takes, and model/version used.

Prompt Architecture

The still-image prompt describes identity and composition. The video prompt assumes the approved frame already contains those facts and describes only motion.

Keyframe prompt

SHOT: EP01_SC03_SH020 FORMAT: 16:9 storyboard keyframe, clean stylized 3D animation REFERENCES: preserve Scraps v07 and Kitchen Counter v03 exactly COMPOSITION: medium close-up, Scraps frame-left, lonely carrot frame-right ACTION STATE: Scraps has just noticed the carrot; curious, gentle expression CAMERA: eye level, 50 mm feel, shallow depth separation LIGHT: warm morning kitchen light, soft purple fridge spill CONTINUITY: carrot remains beside the blue bowl; Scraps wears no added clothing NEGATIVE: no text, no extra fingers, no duplicate props, no redesign, no open mouth dialogue pose

Image-to-video motion prompt

Preserve the supplied image, character design, lighting, composition, and prop positions. Over four seconds, Scraps notices the carrot, blinks once, then leans forward slightly with gentle curiosity. His paws stay planted. The carrot and bowl do not move. Camera: extremely slow push-in only. No pan, tilt, roll, reframing, lens change, or cut. Motion: restrained feature-animation timing; subtle breathing and ear movement. Do not add objects, alter anatomy, change facial design, open the mouth for speech, or change the background.

Budget Targets & Guardrails

The real metric is approved seconds per dollar—not generations, credits, or advertised maximum duration.

Prototype month — about $18–$40

Runway Standard or Luma Plus + ElevenLabs Starter + Resolve Free. Produce a 20–30 second proof containing dialogue-free acting, prop interaction, a camera move, and one continuity cut. This exposes the actual hard parts before committing to an episode.

Episode production month — about $36–$120+

Luma Plus or higher, ElevenLabs Starter/Creator, plus a reserve for a second model on failed hero shots. The range depends less on final runtime than shot difficulty and retry rate.

Green-light metrics

  • At least 60% of simple shots approved within three generations.
  • No more than 15% of shots require paint fixes or replacement inserts.
  • Characters remain recognizable across five consecutive cuts.
  • Cost and labor per approved second are recorded after every batch.

When not to regenerate

  • Use a hold, crop, reaction insert, cutaway, mask, or 2.5D parallax when story clarity is intact.
  • Split complex action into anticipation / action / reaction shots.
  • Reserve premium generations for food beauty shots, emotional close-ups, and signature gags.
  • Never upscale rejected motion; resolution cannot repair bad acting or continuity.
